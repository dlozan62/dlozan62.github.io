Candidate ideas

The five candidate ideas

  1. 01

    Real-time food availability

    Students sometimes travel to a campus dining location expecting a specific item, only to find out that it is sold out. This can waste time or cause them to spend more money on another meal.

    Candidate idea

    Show whether items and stations are available, low stock, or sold out.

  2. 02

    Dining hours and service status

    Posted hours do not always show whether every part of a dining location is operating. A location may be open even when its kitchen, counter, or equipment is unavailable.

    Candidate idea

    Show separate hours and live status for kitchens and counters.

  3. 03

    Meal-plan rollover

    Students are concerned about losing meal-plan money when they do not use it before it expires.

    Candidate idea

    Let unused money roll over and clearly show the remaining balance.

  4. 04

    Customizable meal plans

    Students have different budgets and eating habits, so one meal-plan structure may not work well for everyone.

    Candidate idea

    Offer dollar-balance, set-meal, or combined plan options.

  5. 05

    Dining alerts

    Students often learn about sold-out items, restocking problems, and equipment failures only after they arrive.

    Candidate idea

    Notify students when an item, station, or service becomes unavailable.

Scoping decision

Selected direction

Real-Time Campus Dining Availability System

One of the most common problems was that students expected an item or service to be available, but found out otherwise when they arrived. Students reported sold-out ribs and soup, missing sandwiches, empty grab-and-go cases, unavailable milk options, cleared stations, and a kitchen that closed before the café. These problems caused detours, delays, substitutions, hunger, and extra spending.

Because this issue appeared in several interviews and had clear consequences, our team chose to focus on current item, station, and service information that students can check before walking to a dining location.

Interview evidence

What students experienced

Students often found out after they arrived

Several students only learned about a change after reaching the counter, seeing an empty case, or reading a paper sign. The website or menu they checked beforehand did not show the update.

  • A café was open, but its kitchen was closed. One participant spent $18 and waited roughly 40 minutes for a delivery to the lab.
  • An empty grab-and-go case caused one participant to take a 25-minute detour across campus for pizza.
  • When the ribs were sold out, one participant spent about $6 extra on a snack and waited until after class for a full meal.
  • Sold-out soup, missing sandwiches, unavailable oat milk, and cleared stations caused several students to choose something else.

The information was sometimes accurate

Several participants found that posted hours or menus matched reality. The problem did not happen every time. Our goal is to communicate the changes that regular menu and hours pages may not show.

Research protocol

How we conducted the interviews

The focused interview

The availability protocol asked about the last time a participant checked a menu or hours, what was actually available on arrival, the most recent surprise, what they did next, and the time or money it cost.

Mom Test rules

We asked participants about specific past experiences instead of pitching a solution. We listened for repeated problems, workarounds, consequences, and examples that did not support our project idea.

What the packet contains

The submitted PDF includes 25 Phase 2 response sheets. Twelve use the focused menu-and-availability questions, while 13 cover meal-plan preferences such as price, rollover, payment timing, and plan structure. We kept these two groups separate on this page.

First-draft business objective

Help students avoid wasted trips and extra costs

Our first objective is to find out whether current information about items, stations, and services can reduce wasted trips, delays, substitutions, and unexpected spending when students choose where to eat on campus.

Next validation questions

  • Which status changes are important enough to publish or notify?
  • How quickly must availability data be updated to remain trustworthy?
  • Who can report and verify changes without adding excessive staff work?
  • Should we measure the value through time, cost, or meal disruption?
