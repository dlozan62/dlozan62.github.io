Candidate ideas

Five problems worth testing

  1. 01

    Real-time food availability

    Students can travel to a dining location expecting a specific item, only to discover that it is sold out. The result can be wasted time, an unwanted substitution, or added cost.

    Signal

    Show items and stations as available, low stock, or sold out.

  2. 02

    Dining hours and service status

    Posted hours can say that a location is open without showing that a kitchen, counter, or piece of equipment is already unavailable.

    Signal

    Separate location hours from live kitchen and counter status.

  3. 03

    Meal-plan rollover

    Students worry about paying for meal-plan value that disappears when it is not used before an expiration date.

    Signal

    Carry unused value forward and make the remaining balance clear.

  4. 04

    Customizable meal plans

    Different budgets and eating habits make a single fixed plan a poor fit for some students.

    Signal

    Offer dollar-balance, set-meal, or hybrid structures.

  5. 05

    Dining alerts

    Students often learn about sold-out items, restocking problems, and equipment failures only after they arrive.

    Signal

    Notify students when a meaningful availability change occurs.

Scoping decision

Selected direction

Real-Time Campus Dining Availability System

The strongest repeated problem was the gap between what students expected and what was actually available when they arrived. Interviewees reported sold-out ribs and soup, missing sandwiches, empty grab-and-go cases, unavailable milk options, cleared stations, and a kitchen that closed before the café. These failures led to detours, delays, substitutions, hunger, and extra spending.

Because the pattern appeared across independent conversations and carried concrete consequences, the team chose to focus on current item, station, and service information before a student walks to a dining location.

Interview evidence

What happened in practice

Availability failed at the point of arrival

The focused interviews repeatedly show students discovering a change at the counter, in an empty case, or from a paper sign—not in the digital source they checked beforehand.

  • $18 and 40 minutes: a café was open, but its kitchen was closed, so one participant ordered delivery to the lab.
  • 25-minute detour: an empty grab-and-go case sent one participant across campus for pizza.
  • $6 extra: sold-out ribs led one participant to buy a snack and wait until after class for a full meal.
  • Repeated substitutions: sold-out soup, missing sandwiches, unavailable oat milk, and cleared stations changed what students ate.

Not every experience failed

Several participants found that posted hours or menus matched reality. That disconfirming evidence matters: the opportunity is not to replace every menu, but to communicate the changes that static information misses.

Research protocol

Behavior before opinion

The focused interview

The availability protocol asked about the last time a participant checked a menu or hours, what was actually available on arrival, the most recent surprise, what they did next, and the time or money it cost.

Mom Test rules

Interviewers discussed the participant’s life instead of pitching a solution, asked about specific past behavior, and listened for repeated problems, workarounds, consequences, and evidence that challenged the emerging direction.

What the packet contains

The submitted PDF includes 25 Phase 2 response sheets: 12 use the focused menu-and-availability protocol, while 13 capture meal-plan preferences such as price, rollover, payment timing, and plan structure. This page keeps those evidence streams distinct.

First-draft business objective

Reduce avoidable dining disruption

Validate whether timely item-, station-, and service-level availability information can reduce wasted trips, delays, substitutions, and unexpected spending for students choosing where to eat on campus.

Next validation questions

  • Which status changes are important enough to publish or notify?
  • How quickly must availability data be updated to remain trustworthy?
  • Who can report and verify changes without adding excessive staff work?
  • Which consequence—time, cost, or meal disruption—best measures value?
