Business case

Who would gain from DineCheck and what the first slice would cost, then what could go wrong. Our go/no-go recommendation is at the end.

What is being decided

The request is to build one slice of DineCheck: students can see which dining locations are open, what is on each menu, and which items are sold out, and dining staff get a form to mark items sold out. The slice is defined in the estimation appendix.

It would take an expected 694 team hours (range 294 to 1,150) and a budget request of $45,919. Crowd levels, wait times, point-of-sale integration, and a campus-wide launch are not part of this decision.

Students find out after they arrive

Students often learn a dining location is closed or an item is sold out only after they get there. In our twelve focused interviews from Sprint 1, one student found a cafe open after its kitchen had closed and spent about $18 on delivery. Another walked 25 minutes across campus for pizza after finding an empty grab-and-go case. A third paid about $6 for a snack when the ribs ran out.

Posted hours and menus were sometimes right, but they don't show what changes during the day, like an item running out over lunch or a counter closing early. Those changes are what DineCheck is for.

Twelve interviews show the problem exists. They don't show how often it happens across campus. See Sprint 1 market research.

Money is the weak part of this case. Priced only as student time at minimum wage, the slice returns $40,781 over three years against a three-year cost of $59,959, an ROI of −32%. It breaks even at about 735 weekly users. The full numbers are in the ROI analysis.

The value we can't price yet is trust: whether students believe the status enough to change where they walk. The charter's prototype and usability gates are set up to test that.

Go/no-go

Go, with conditions

The team decided to go ahead with the slice and project. It seems feasible, useful and straightforward. Let's build on the condition that we stress test and manage a close collaboration with the university and the restaurant on campus, because success depends on staff updating the menu inventory and times. Another business idea is passing the costs to the university as a service rather than the students.

For Method 1, the middle value is: (294 + 588) ÷ 2 = 441 hours

For Method 2, the middle value is: (690 + 1,150) ÷ 2 = 920 hours, the same as 46 UCP × 20 hours.

Gap 920 ÷ 441 = 2.09

Do your two methods depend on the same assumption? No. Function Points depend on the system's inputs, outputs, inquiries, stored data, and productivity rate, while Use-Case Points depend on the actors, use-case complexity, and hours per UCP.

Which single input moves the answer most? The Function Point productivity rate moves the answer the most. At 6 hours per FP, the estimate is 294 hours, while at 12 hours per FP, it increases to 588 hours. For use-case points, moving from 15 to 25 hours per UCP takes the estimate from 690 to 1,150 hours.

What information would narrow the gap, and when could you get it? Actual team productivity data would narrow the gap. Once development begins, we could measure the actual hours needed to complete features and use cases instead of relying on assumed productivity rates.

Our explanation of the gap: The Function Point method produced an estimate of 294–588 hours with a middle value of 441 hours, while the Use-Case Point method produced 690–1,150 hours with a middle value of 920 hours. The gap between the middle values is about 2.09; it was 1.38 before we added the dining-staff actor. Adding the staff side added one input to the function points (+4 FP) but an actor and two use cases to the use-case points (+18 UCP), and use-case points give every use case at least 5 points even when it is a small lookup. Both methods also rely on assumed productivity rates because we do not yet have actual development data. Collecting real productivity data during development would help reduce this uncertainty.
