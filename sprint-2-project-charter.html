Project decision and evidence

The team selected a real-time campus dining availability system after comparing food availability, dining hours and service status, meal-plan rollover, customizable meal plans, and dining alerts. The selected direction is to test current status information for dining items, stations, kitchens, counters, and temporary service changes.

  • 12 focused availability interviews: primary evidence for the current direction.
  • 13 meal-plan preference responses: background from the earlier direction, not validation for the availability system.
  • Five candidate ideas: options compared before the project was narrowed.

Problem statement and business objective

Students sometimes arrive at a campus dining location and discover that an item, station, kitchen service, counter, or required option is unavailable. When earlier information does not show the change, they may lose time, substitute a meal, remain hungry, or spend more than planned.

The first business objective is to find out whether current information can help students avoid wasted trips, delays, substitutions, and unexpected spending when they decide where to eat on campus.

Scope boundary

In scope

  • 13 additional focused interviews
  • Functional and nonfunctional requirements
  • Status and outage definitions
  • Update and verification workflow
  • Low-fidelity and interactive prototypes
  • Usability tests and feasibility findings

Out of scope

  • Production website or mobile app
  • Live UTEP or vendor integrations
  • Guaranteed real-time accuracy
  • Staffing, policy, pricing, payment, or rollover changes
  • Replacement of official systems
  • Unapproved staff or vendor commitments

Deferred

  • Production application development
  • Menu, inventory, or point-of-sale integrations
  • Push notifications and user accounts
  • Analytics and role administration
  • A limited pilot and campus-wide rollout
  • Operational ownership and service commitments

Project approach

  1. Validation: reach 25 focused availability interviews and decide whether the problem is repeated, specific, and costly enough to continue.
  2. Requirements: rank needed status changes, define freshness, and identify who could report and verify each change.
  3. Prototype: build a testable flow that shows location, availability, status details, and when information was updated.
  4. Usability and feasibility: test with students and review operational limits before considering a pilot proposal.

Assumptions and dependencies

  • Students will check availability before walking to a dining location.
  • Available, Low Stock, Sold Out, Closed, and Service Outage will be clear enough to support a decision.
  • Availability can be updated often enough to remain useful.
  • An approved source can report changes without excessive work.
  • Students from different groups experience the problem.
  • The project can show stale or uncertain information honestly.

Risks and planned responses

  • Stale information: show update times, define stale states, and test verification paths.
  • Staff workload: test the shortest update flow and compare possible reporting approaches.
  • No live-system access: use realistic sample data and document integration questions.
  • Research sample bias: recruit across student types, schedules, and dining locations.
  • Scope growth: use this boundary and record requests before adding work.
  • Unclear ownership: do not recommend a pilot until reporting, verification, and correction responsibilities are documented.
  • Accessibility and privacy: use readable labels, keyboard-friendly interactions, color-independent cues, and minimal participant data.

Proposed success criteria and stage gates

  • Reach 25 focused availability interviews while keeping meal-plan responses separate.
  • Document each status, data source, owner, freshness expectation, and stale-state behavior.
  • Let a student identify whether an item or service is Available, Low Stock, Sold Out, Closed, or affected by a Service Outage.
  • Test with at least five participants, with at least four completing the main task without help.
  • Document a realistic update owner, workflow, and data source before recommending a pilot.

Governance and open decisions

Team 10 reviews research findings and scope changes together because no project lead has been chosen and responsibility is shared.

  • Which changes are important enough to publish or notify?
  • How quickly must each type of information be updated?
  • Who can report and verify a change without excessive staff work?
  • Should value be measured through time saved, cost avoided, or fewer disrupted meals?
