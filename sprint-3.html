01 · Listen

Student interviews

Three records about time pressure, suitable meals, and familiar routines. Open an interview to read all seven questions and responses.

Interview 1 — Student with a tight class schedule7 questions

1. Think about the last time you needed food between campus activities. Walk us through your decision, from first considering food to getting your meal.

“I had 35 minutes between classes and hadn’t eaten. I considered the campus food court and a restaurant across the street. I chose the food court because it was closer to my next classroom. When I arrived, the counter I wanted had a long line, so I bought a packaged sandwich.”

2. What mattered most in that situation, and what did you compromise on?

“Time mattered most. I compromised on the meal I wanted because missing class wasn’t worth it.”

3. Show us how you usually find the information you need before choosing where to eat. At what point do you know enough to make a decision?

“For a familiar location, I usually don’t look anything up. For somewhere unfamiliar, I check the university dining page for hours and use a map to estimate the walking distance.”

4. Tell us about a time you could not confirm something important before leaving. How did that uncertainty affect what you did?

“Once, I didn’t know whether a counter was still serving lunch. I went anyway because checking another source would have taken time, and there were other counters nearby.”

5. Describe a specific time when the hours, menu, or food availability differed from what you expected. What happened afterward?

“I walked to a cafe that was open, but its kitchen had stopped serving food. I spent about 12 minutes getting there and back, then went to class without a meal.”

6. Tell us about a time two sources gave you different dining information. How did you decide which one to trust?

“A maps listing showed a cafe as open, but the university page listed shorter hours. I followed the university page because I assumed it accounted for the campus schedule.”

7. What does your current method do well, and what problem would a new tool have to solve for you to change it?

“My current method is quick because I know the campus. A new tool would need to help me avoid a trip that won’t work within my break.”

Interview 2 — Student balancing budget and dietary preferences7 questions

1. Think about the last time you needed food between campus activities. Walk us through your decision, from first considering food to getting your meal.

“I had a longer break and wanted a vegetarian meal. I checked the campus menu and saw a bowl that looked suitable. When I arrived, it was sold out. I compared the remaining options with those at a nearby restaurant and decided to eat off campus.”

2. What mattered most in that situation, and what did you compromise on?

“Finding a suitable, filling meal mattered most. I compromised on price and convenience because the restaurant was farther away and cost more.”

3. Show us how you usually find the information you need before choosing where to eat. At what point do you know enough to make a decision?

“I check menus and compare prices. If the ingredients are unclear, I ask staff. I know enough to decide when I find something that meets my preferences and fits my budget.”

4. Tell us about a time you could not confirm something important before leaving. How did that uncertainty affect what you did?

“I couldn’t find the ingredients for a sauce on the online menu. I chose another meal because I didn’t want to arrive and discover that my first choice wasn’t suitable.”

5. Describe a specific time when the hours, menu, or food availability differed from what you expected. What happened afterward?

“The vegetarian bowl was listed online but sold out when I arrived. I walked to a restaurant nearby, spent about $4 more than planned, and used most of my break getting lunch.”

6. Tell us about a time two sources gave you different dining information. How did you decide which one to trust?

“The online menu and a sign at the counter described an item differently. I asked an employee to confirm the ingredients because neither description gave me enough confidence.”

7. What does your current method do well, and what problem would a new tool have to solve for you to change it?

“My current method helps me compare meals and prices. A new tool would need accurate ingredient information and current availability so I could choose a suitable meal before walking over.”

Interview 3 — Student who prefers familiar routines7 questions

1. Think about the last time you needed food between campus activities. Walk us through your decision, from first considering food to getting your meal.

“After my morning class, I went to the campus cafe I usually visit. I didn’t check anything online because it was on my way. When I arrived, I looked at the display and bought coffee and a pastry.”

2. What mattered most in that situation, and what did you compromise on?

“Convenience mattered most. I was willing to choose from whatever was available instead of going somewhere else for a particular item.”

3. Show us how you usually find the information you need before choosing where to eat. At what point do you know enough to make a decision?

“For familiar places, I rely on my routine. For somewhere new, I use a maps app to check directions and hours. Knowing that it is nearby and should be open is usually enough.”

4. Tell us about a time you could not confirm something important before leaving. How did that uncertainty affect what you did?

“I wasn’t sure whether the cafe would be open during a school break. I walked past it anyway because it was already on my route, and I could get coffee elsewhere if it was closed.”

5. Describe a specific time when the hours, menu, or food availability differed from what you expected. What happened afterward?

“One afternoon, the cafe was open but had no pastries left. I bought coffee and ate something from my bag. It wasn’t a major inconvenience because I wasn’t looking for a specific meal.”

6. Tell us about a time two sources gave you different dining information. How did you decide which one to trust?

“A maps listing showed normal hours, but a campus announcement showed holiday hours. I trusted the announcement because it referred to that particular date.”

7. What does your current method do well, and what problem would a new tool have to solve for you to change it?

“My current method takes very little effort and usually works. I would use a new tool when I wanted a specific item or needed to check unusual hours, but I probably wouldn’t check it before every visit.”

Competitor 1: UTEP Dining Website

Type: Direct competitor and existing campus alternative.

What it offers: The portal lists dining locations and hours and includes a “What’s Open Now” section. The Pick N’ Shovel page also provides a menu date selector, nutrition information, and dietary and allergen filters.

Walkthrough observation: I opened the hours page, selected Pick N’ Shovel, changed the menu date, and enabled the vegetarian filter. The filter accepted the selection, but the reviewed menu area did not provide enough item information to confirm current availability.

Strength: It already brings campus-specific information together. DineCheck cannot claim that campus hours or dietary filters are unique features.

Weakness: The reviewed menu area did not clearly distinguish scheduled menu items from food currently available at the counter. There was not enough item information to verify availability before visiting.

Question to investigate: Can students distinguish a scheduled menu from food that is actually available at the counter?

Possible DineCheck advantage: Clearly distinguish location open, counter serving, and item available, with visible update times. This advantage still needs testing.

Where DineCheck falls short: UTEP Dining is the official campus source and students already use it to check hours. DineCheck has not yet shown a dependable staff update process or that students would open a second tool.

Competitor 2: Nutrislice

Type: Direct competitor offering digital dining menus.

What it offers: The Nutrislice app provides daily menus, food descriptions, nutrition information, allergen and dietary filters, and favorite locations. The University of New Hampshire's published descriptions also list a nutrition calculator.

Walkthrough observation: UNH's public menu stayed blank in a browser. Team 10 then installed the Nutrislice app on mobile devices, and menu data loaded as soon as the University of New Hampshire location was selected in the iOS app. No login was required and no pricing was offered. The walkthrough record does not establish whether sold-out items or update times were shown.

Strength: Its menus, nutrition tools, and filters overlap with DineCheck's proposed features. DineCheck cannot claim these features are unique.

Weakness to investigate: Our walkthrough record does not yet establish whether students can distinguish scheduled menu items from food currently available at the counter. This is an evidence gap, not a confirmed platform weakness.

Question to investigate: Does a campus implementation clearly communicate sold-out items, when availability was last updated, and when information may be outdated?

Possible DineCheck advantage: A simpler, campus-specific workflow that clearly separates location open, counter serving, and item available, with visible update times. This advantage still needs testing.

Where DineCheck falls short: DineCheck is currently a proposed solution and has not demonstrated dependable availability updates or a working staff update process. It also has not shown that its workflow is easier to use than Nutrislice’s existing tools.

Competitor 3: Google Maps

Type: Indirect competitor; a general restaurant-discovery and navigation alternative.

What it offers: Google Maps helps users find restaurants and get directions. Restaurant profiles can include opening status, hours, addresses, reviews, website links, and menu information.

Walkthrough observation: I searched for restaurants near UTEP and opened Crave Kitchen & Bar. The profile displayed opening status, hours, an address, reviews, directions, and a restaurant website link. The session also displayed a limited-view notice, which restricted the review.

Strength: It combines restaurant discovery with practical information students need to choose and reach a location, including options on and off campus.

Weakness: The reviewed profile did not establish whether a particular food item was currently available. Opening status and general restaurant information alone were insufficient to confirm item availability.

Question to investigate: Can students confirm that a particular food item is available without leaving Maps or contacting the restaurant?

Possible DineCheck advantage: Staff-confirmed campus item availability presented alongside dining hours and visible update times. A campus-specific workflow could help students decide where to eat between classes, but this advantage still needs testing.

Where DineCheck falls short: DineCheck’s proposed scope covers campus dining locations. Google Maps supports a broader search for restaurants on and off campus and combines discovery with directions and reviews. DineCheck has also not yet demonstrated a dependable process for keeping item availability current.

Team 10 – DineCheck Competitors

Competitor Teardown — UTEP Dining

Competitor: UTEP Dining Website

Type: Direct Competitor / Existing Campus Alternative

Website: https://utepdining.sodexomyway.com/en-us/

Duration: 30 minutes

Conducted by Team 10 on mobile devices; no account is needed for access.

1. Overview

UTEP Dining is a website that provides students with information about dining locations on campus. Students can use it to check operating hours, browse menus, and review nutritional and dietary information.

It is a direct competitor to DineCheck because both aim to help students make decisions about where and what to eat on campus.

2. Features and Observations

During the teardown, we opened the UTEP Dining hours page and selected Pick N' Shovel to explore its dining information.

We changed the menu date and enabled the vegetarian dietary filter. The website accepted the filter selection, but the menu section we reviewed did not provide enough information to determine whether specific food items were currently available. The most recent menu visible was from July, so the posted menu was out of date. The website is free to use.

3. Strengths

Provides information specifically about UTEP dining locations.

Displays operating hours for campus dining options.

Includes menu and nutritional information.

Offers dietary and allergen filters.

Combines campus dining information in one website.

4. Limitations and Areas to Investigate

Although the website provides useful information, our review raised questions about how accurately students can determine current food availability.

Areas requiring further investigation include:

Whether students can identify sold-out menu items.

Whether the website distinguishes between a dining location being open and its kitchen still serving food.

Whether students can see when menu availability was last updated.

Whether students can confirm that a particular meal is available before walking to the location.

These are areas for further testing rather than confirmed missing features.

5. Comparison to DineCheck

UTEP Dining already provides menus, operating hours, and dietary filters, meaning DineCheck cannot treat these features as unique advantages.

DineCheck could potentially differentiate itself by emphasizing current food availability. For example, the proposed application could show whether a dining location is open, whether food is still being served, and whether specific items are available.

This could help students avoid unnecessary trips during short breaks between classes.

6. Conclusion

UTEP Dining provides useful information for students searching for food on campus. However, our walkthrough did not establish whether students can reliably confirm the availability of specific menu items, and the most recent menu we could see was from July.

DineCheck could potentially improve this experience by presenting clearer, more frequently verified availability information.

A visible update time and current menus would address the two problems we found. DineCheck would still need to show that its information stays more current than the official site.

Competitor Teardown 3 — Nutrislice

Competitor: Nutrislice

Type: Direct Competitor

Duration: 30 minutes

Conducted by Team 10 on mobile devices through the Nutrislice iOS app. No login was required, and no pricing was offered; the app is free to use, just location that is compatible is needed.

Location used: University of New Hampshire

1. Overview

Nutrislice is a digital dining platform used by universities to display food menus and nutritional information. It allows students to check dining options, review ingredients, and filter foods based on dietary needs.

Nutrislice is a direct competitor to DineCheck because both focus on helping students find dining information and make decisions about what to eat.

2. Features

During the walkthrough, Nutrislice provided the following features:

Daily dining menus.

Food descriptions and nutritional information.

Dietary and allergen filters.

Favorite dining locations.

Menu updates by dining staff through its Sidekick application (from Nutrislice's documentation; not tested by us).

These features overlap with several functions that DineCheck plans to offer.

3. Walkthrough

We first tried the University of New Hampshire's public Nutrislice menu in a browser.

The menu stayed blank in the browser. We then installed the Nutrislice iOS app and selected the University of New Hampshire location, and the menu data loaded immediately.

No login was required and no pricing was offered.

4. Strengths

Provides detailed nutritional information.

Includes dietary and allergen filtering options.

Allows universities to organize dining menus digitally.

Supports menu updates by dining staff.

Already offers several features similar to DineCheck.

5. Limitations and Questions to Investigate

Does Nutrislice clearly indicate when a particular food item is sold out?

Can students identify when menu information was last updated?

Can users distinguish between scheduled menu items and currently available food?

How easy is it to find a specific meal using dietary filters?

Can students rely on the information when making last-minute dining decisions?

Any of these questions not answered during the walkthrough still require testing. They should not be treated as confirmed weaknesses.

6. Comparison to DineCheck

Nutrislice already provides digital menus, nutritional information, and dietary filters. Because of this, DineCheck cannot claim these features are unique.

One potential way DineCheck could differentiate itself is by making current food availability easier for students to verify.

For example, DineCheck could distinguish between a location being open, a counter serving food, and a specific menu item being available.

However, further testing is required to determine whether Nutrislice already provides similar capabilities.

7. Conclusion

Nutrislice is an established competitor with several features that overlap with DineCheck's proposed functionality. Our research suggests that DineCheck would need to offer more than digital menus and dietary filters to stand out. Our initial suggestions are easier navigation, simpler use, and a clearer visual design.

Contradiction Memo

Assumption Being Challenged

An assumption worth challenging in our business case is that students will check DineCheck regularly before choosing where to eat. Our proposed system depends on students finding enough value in current dining information to add another step to their routine.

The interviews suggest that this value depends on the situation. Some students face meaningful consequences when information is wrong, while others can adapt without much inconvenience.

Contradicting Evidence

Interview 3 provides the clearest contradiction. The student usually visits a familiar cafe because it is already along their route. They do not check online before regular visits and choose from whatever is available. When pastries were unavailable, they bought coffee and ate food from their bag. They considered this a minor inconvenience.

The student said they might use a new tool when looking for a specific item or checking unusual hours, but probably would not check it before every visit. This suggests that unavailable food does not always create a strong enough problem to change an established habit.

Interview 1 also complicates the assumption. That student sometimes visits without confirming service because checking takes time. Even a student who has experienced an unsuccessful trip may skip an information tool if using it feels slower than proceeding with a familiar option.

Why This Matters

These findings do not show that DineCheck lacks value. They show that experiencing a dining inconvenience does not automatically lead to regular use.

Interview 1 described losing about 12 minutes after visiting an open cafe whose kitchen had stopped serving. Interview 2 described a sold-out vegetarian bowl that led to more walking and about $4 in additional spending. Those situations provide stronger reasons to check availability than a routine visit where several acceptable alternatives exist.

If we assume frequent use across the student population, we may overestimate adoption and the benefits used to justify development and maintenance costs. We should distinguish students who need reliable information for a particular decision from students whose current routines already work well.

My Advice

I recommend that we focus the initial evaluation on three situations: short breaks between classes, choosing a specific or suitable meal, and checking unusual operating hours. These situations give students a concrete reason to seek information before leaving.

We should compare DineCheck with participants’ current methods and measure whether it improves the decision. Useful measures include time needed to find an acceptable option, correct interpretation of serving status, and whether participants return to the tool without prompting.

Until testing supports a stronger claim, our business case should describe DineCheck as a tool for reducing uncertainty in particular dining situations. We should treat regular use as a hypothesis to test rather than an expected outcome.

Assumption Being Challenged

Our business case that came out of our Sprint 2 frames a problem that students are finding out an item is sold out after they arrive. The first slice is being built around that: students see which locations are open, which items are available/sold out, and staff can get a digital dashboard or form that can be used to mark items sold out. The assumption is that “open” plus “sold out” is the information students are mostly missing and would find the most helpful.

The interviews suggest that sold-out status is one of several gaps but not the most common one to consider maybe.

Contradicting Evidence

Interview 1 shows this in the clearest case; when the student walked to a cafe that was open, but its kitchen had already stopped serving food. The 12-minute walk and back to class without eating, shows that while nothing was sold out, and the location was open, the counter wasn’t serving. In our current slice, DineCheck would have shown this cafe as open with items, which would have still affected the student in a negative way regardless. The same student also mentioned going to a counter and not knowing “if they are still serving lunch.”

Interview 2 partly supports our assumption, as the vegetarian bowl was listed online but sold out. The same student’s other problem was the ingredients. They couldn’t find the ingredients for a sauce online, so they were forced to eat a different meal. When the online menu and the counter sign described the item differently, asking an employee because neither of them was trustworthy.

Interview 3 did run into a sold-out item since the cafe had no desserts left, but the student called it minor and just went to a coffee shop. The real issue being hours that change on specific dates. The maps listing showed normal hours while campus announcements showed holiday hours, and the student would not be sure cafe would be open during coffee break. Being a graduate student at this university I have had experience with emails that denote extended or special hours for dining options in campus, but (again in my personal experience) these have not always been consistent; so this interview was of particular note to me as I had failed to consider this.

Across three interviews, the one sold out case cost a student something was the vegetarian bowl. Other problems that mattered were counter stopped serving while location was technically open, unclear ingredients and special university hours/events.

Why this Matters

If we build only "open" and "sold-out" as options, DineCheck can still send students on the trip that failed in Interview 1. That is the problem we are trying to solve, and it also weakens our main difference from the UTEP Dining site, which already shows what's open and has menus and dietary filters. It also affects the estimate. Our function points and use cases count one staff input (mark sold out). A counter stopped serving status is a small addition, but ingredient accuracy is a bigger content problem that our slice doesn't cover, and our budget doesn't price.

My Advice

I recommend we change the slice's status model from two states to three: location open, counter serving, and item available, each with the time it was last updated. Staff would mark a counter as "stopped serving" with the same one-tap action we planned for sold-out items, so the extra cost should be small (no more training). We should re-check the function point count before claiming that.

Ingredient information should be recorded as a known need outside this slice, not quietly added. Holiday and break hours should be tested against the UTEP site first, since it may already handle them. Before the next sprint, we should ask in our next interviews which of these problems happened most recently and what they cost them. Until then, the business case should say students can't confirm what is actually being served rather than items sold out.

Candidate Risk Entries

Risk 1 Inaccurate or Outdated Availability

Risk: If dining staff cannot consistently update counter and item status, DineCheck may display incorrect information, causing unsuccessful trips and reducing student trust.

Evidence: Interview 1 described an open cafe whose kitchen had stopped serving. Interview 2 described a meal listed online that was sold out upon arrival.

Trigger: During a pilot, an item marked available is unavailable when checked, a counter marked serving has stopped service, or information exceeds the team’s agreed freshness limit.

Proposed owner: Dining operations liaison.

Mitigation: Agree with participating staff on update responsibilities and a realistic update frequency. Keep updates simple, display the last update time, and mark information as unconfirmed when it exceeds the freshness limit. Check displayed status against actual conditions during the pilot.

Risk 2 Students Do Not Adopt the Tool

Risk: If checking DineCheck adds effort without improving dining decisions, students may continue using familiar routines and existing services.

Evidence: Interview 3 described a student who rarely checks information before familiar visits and would probably use a new tool only for a specific item or unusual hours.

Trigger: In pilot tasks, students repeatedly skip DineCheck, report no useful advantage, or do not return without prompting.

Proposed owner: User research lead.

Mitigation: Test the tool with students facing short breaks, specific meal preferences, or unusual hours. Compare it with their current methods on the same tasks. Measure decision time, task success, and unprompted repeat use before expanding the scope.

Risk 3 Insufficient Advantage Over Existing Alternatives

Risk: If DineCheck mainly duplicates existing hours, menus, and dietary filters, its benefits may not justify the development and maintenance effort.

Evidence: The competitor review identified overlapping features in UTEP Dining and Nutrislice. Google Maps already supports discovery, hours, directions, and reviews. Nutrislice’s handling of current availability remains unverified.

Trigger: Further walkthroughs show that an existing service already meets the target need, or comparative testing shows no meaningful improvement from DineCheck.

Proposed owner: Product lead.

Mitigation: Complete live competitor walkthroughs focused on serving status, sold-out items, update times, and outdated information. Test DineCheck against the strongest existing alternative. Narrow, integrate, or reconsider the proposed solution if a useful advantage cannot be demonstrated.

Change Note

Sprint 3 evidence supports refining DineCheck’s business case around dependable dining availability information. The proposed changes below respond to the interview findings and competitor reviews. They do not establish that the proposed benefits have been achieved.

Clarify the Problem DineCheck Addresses

The interviews show that a location being open does not necessarily mean its kitchen is serving or a desired item is available. Interview 1 described an unsuccessful trip to an open cafe whose kitchen had stopped serving. Interview 2 described a vegetarian bowl listed online that was sold out upon arrival.

The business case should distinguish three conditions: location open, counter serving, and item available. This makes the proposed problem and solution more specific than a general need for dining hours or online menus.

Refine the Competitive Advantage

The competitor review shows that existing alternatives already provide many proposed features. UTEP Dining offers campus hours, menus, and dietary filters. Nutrislice’s documented features include menus, nutrition information, and dietary tools. Google Maps supports restaurant discovery, hours, directions, and reviews.

The business case should therefore present dependable item availability and clear serving status as potential advantages requiring validation. Campus specificity, menus, and filters alone do not establish meaningful differentiation.

Narrow the Initial Use Cases

Interview 3 challenges the assumption that students need to check dining information before every visit. The student generally followed a familiar routine and considered unavailable pastries a minor inconvenience.

The business case should prioritize students facing a short break, a specific food requirement, or unusual operating hours. Expected use should be evaluated around these situations rather than assuming frequent use by every student.

Make Information Maintenance Part of Feasibility

DineCheck’s value depends on whether students can trust the information before leaving. Incorrect or outdated status could reproduce the same unsuccessful trips the project aims to prevent.

The feasibility assessment should include how staff update status, who owns that responsibility, and how the system communicates outdated or uncertain information. Visible update times should support this process, but they cannot substitute for accurate updates.

Treat Benefits as Measurable Hypotheses

The interviews identify possible benefits, including fewer unsuccessful trips, less time spent searching, and better meal choices. They do not establish campus-wide demand, adoption rates, or financial returns.

The business case should describe these benefits as hypotheses. Evaluation should measure whether students correctly interpret availability, make decisions more quickly, and find enough value to use the tool again.

Effect on Scope Budget and Schedule

These findings support clarifying the initial scope and validation priorities. They do not, by themselves, justify changing the existing budget or schedule.

Before revising estimates, the team should determine what the staff update workflow requires and whether it changes development effort or operating responsibilities. Any resulting cost or schedule changes should be documented separately with their assumptions.

Competitive Landscape

Competitive Positioning Map

The proposed map uses two dimensions: campus specificity, ranging from general restaurant information to campus-focused dining information, and clarity of current item availability, ranging from scheduled menus or general hours to confirmed item status with visible update times.

UTEP Dining occupies the campus-focused side. The reviewed pages provided campus locations, hours, menu dates, and dietary filters. Current item availability could not be confirmed during the walkthrough, so its position on that dimension remains uncertain.

Nutrislice supports campus-focused dining through individual university implementations. The reviewed UNH documentation described detailed menus and nutrition tools. Its handling of current availability remains unverified because a live menu walkthrough was not completed.

Google Maps occupies the broader restaurant-discovery side, covering options on and off campus. The reviewed profile provided hours, directions, reviews, and a website link, but did not establish current item availability.

DineCheck’s intended position is campus-focused information with clearly distinguished location, counter, and item status. This is a target position, not a demonstrated capability.

Comparison of Alternatives

UTEP Dining Website

Competitor type: Direct competitor and existing campus alternative.

Capabilities: Campus dining locations, operating hours, a “What’s Open Now” section, menu dates, nutrition information, and dietary and allergen filters.

Strength: It already brings campus-specific dining information together. DineCheck cannot claim that campus hours or dietary filters are unique.

Observed gap: The reviewed menu area did not provide enough information to distinguish scheduled items from food currently available at the counter.

Potential switching reason: Students might choose DineCheck if it provides clearer, dependable item status and update times that help prevent unsuccessful trips.

DineCheck’s disadvantage: The existing campus portal already provides useful information. DineCheck has not demonstrated a reliable staff update process or a better decision-making experience.

Nutrislice

Competitor type: Direct competitor offering digital dining menus.

Capabilities: UNH’s published descriptions included daily menus, food descriptions, nutrition information, dietary and allergen filters, a nutrition calculator, and favorite locations.

Strength: Its documented tools overlap substantially with DineCheck’s proposed features.

Evidence gap: The review did not verify how a live implementation communicates sold-out items, update times, or outdated information. These are questions to investigate rather than confirmed weaknesses.

Potential switching reason: Students might prefer DineCheck if testing demonstrates a quicker workflow and more useful current availability information.

DineCheck’s disadvantage: DineCheck remains a proposed solution. It has not demonstrated that its information is more dependable or its workflow easier than Nutrislice’s tools.

Google Maps

Competitor type: Indirect competitor for restaurant discovery and navigation.

Capabilities: The reviewed Crave Kitchen & Bar profile displayed opening status, hours, an address, reviews, directions, and a restaurant website link.

Strength: It helps students discover and reach dining options both on and off campus.

Observed gap: The reviewed profile did not establish whether a particular food item was currently available.

Potential switching reason: Students might consult DineCheck when they need to confirm a campus food item or counter’s serving status before leaving.

DineCheck’s disadvantage: Its proposed campus scope is narrower. Google Maps also provides directions, reviews, and broader restaurant discovery. Students may use both services rather than replace Maps.

Familiar Routines and Asking Employees

Alternative type: Existing student behavior.

Capabilities: Students rely on familiar locations, inspect food on arrival, or ask employees about ingredients and availability.

Strength: Familiar routines require little effort. Employees can clarify information that online descriptions leave uncertain.

Observed gap: Students may discover a closed kitchen, sold-out item, or unsuitable meal only after arriving.

Potential switching reason: DineCheck could be useful when a student wants a particular item, has a short break, or faces unusual hours.

DineCheck’s disadvantage: Checking another tool adds effort. Interview 3 suggests that minor availability problems may not justify changing an established routine.

Ranked Switching Triggers

These rankings are working priorities based on the three interview records. They are not campus-wide preference rankings or validated adoption results.

1 Preventing a Wasted Trip During a Short Break

Interview 1 described about 12 minutes lost after visiting an open cafe whose kitchen had stopped serving.

Switching trigger: A student needs food within a limited break and cannot confirm that a counter is serving.

Required value: DineCheck must provide useful status quickly enough to improve the decision.

2 Confirming a Suitable Item Before Leaving

Interview 2 described a vegetarian bowl that was listed online but sold out upon arrival. The alternative required more walking and about $4 in additional spending.

Switching trigger: A student wants a suitable meal and needs to know whether it is currently available.

Required value: Availability must be dependable, and ingredient information must be clear enough to support the choice.

3 Resolving Unusual Hours or Conflicting Information

The interviews described conflicts between general hours, campus schedules, and date-specific announcements.

Switching trigger: A holiday, school break, or conflicting listing makes normal hours unreliable.

Required value: DineCheck must clearly identify the applicable date, serving status, and information source.

4 Checking for a Specific Item Outside a Regular Routine

Interview 3 suggested interest in checking a tool for a particular item, while expressing little need to check before ordinary visits.

Switching trigger: A student’s decision depends on a specific item rather than whatever is available.

Required value: The benefit of checking must exceed the effort of opening another tool.

Conditions Needed for Switching

Reliable updates are necessary for every proposed advantage. Visible update times alone are insufficient if the underlying status is inaccurate. DineCheck needs a practical staff update process and a clear way to communicate uncertain or outdated information.

The next evaluation should compare DineCheck with students’ existing methods on the same dining tasks. Useful measures include decision time, correct interpretation of location versus counter status, recognition of outdated information, and whether students return to the tool without prompting.
